# One link. No trail.

How do you give someone a permanent way to send you sensitive files — without that address becoming a record of everyone who ever used it?

Every tool you already use answers that question badly. A shared inbox creates a log. A cloud folder requires an account. A file-sharing link expires, or it doesn't, and either way the platform knows who used it, when, and what they sent. The list exists whether you asked for it or not. And lists, as several large institutions have recently discovered, have a way of ending up somewhere you didn't intend.

Silent Drop is a different answer. You publish one permanent link. Anyone can use it to send you files — no account, no login, nothing that persists past the session. Refueler's server is a blind harbour master: it knows something arrived. It doesn't know which ship, which captain, or what was in the cargo. And because every delivery is cryptographically isolated from every other, there is no common thread connecting sender A to sender B. The map of who sent what to whom doesn't exist, because we never drew it.

---

**The accountant**

A freelance accountant has thirty clients. Each sends documents throughout the year — payslips, bank statements, receipts, things that carry more personal exposure than most people remember when they hit send. Each arrives through whatever channel felt convenient: email, WhatsApp, a link from a folder that's still shared with someone's ex-partner from three jobs ago.

Somewhere, on a server that is someone else's problem, a quiet record exists of who sent what, and when, and to whom. The accountant didn't choose to hold that record. It accumulated, one tax year at a time.

She publishes a Silent Drop link. Each client uses it. Each delivery is cryptographically isolated — different wrapping, no shared identifier connecting one client's transfer to another's. What Refueler logs: something arrived. What Refueler cannot hand over, because we do not hold it: who sent it, what it contained, or that Tuesday's sender and Thursday's sender are the same person.

This is not policy. It is architecture. The distinction matters the moment someone produces paperwork — something any solicitor will confirm, usually with a look that suggests the question should have been asked earlier.

When the tax year ends and a client moves on, there is no residual record of their relationship with her inbox. The link remains. The history does not.

---

**The census**

The problem is older than cloud storage, and simpler.

A hardware manufacturer — makers of well-regarded devices for keeping Bitcoin safe — kept a customer database. Names, home addresses, phone numbers. The Bitcoin stayed safe. The record of who owned Bitcoin did not. It was breached and published online, and in several documented cases people found strangers at their door with a clear sense of what might be inside.

The vault was fine. The census was the weapon.

The damage came not from the files themselves but from the existence of a record connecting identities to activity. A list that was never meant to be a liability, held by an organisation that never meant to cause harm, in a filing cabinet that was simply someone else's problem until it wasn't.

Silent Drop does not make your vault impenetrable. It means the census was never written — no database connecting identity to activity, no list of who sent what to whom. When someone comes for the record, there isn't one.

One link. No trail.
